Chipload 0.39.0 user guide

Moulding

Moulding sweeps a profile along a path: a cove or an ogee round a table top’s face, a bead along a door frame, a round-over beside a raised panel, a decorative band across a lid. You give the path (the line the moulding runs along) and the profile (its cross-section); a ball nose cuts it in passes along the path.

Select the paths (open or closed shapes), then press S over the canvas and M, or choose Operation > New Moulding, or New operation in a part’s right-click menu in the Browser.

A drawn profile is the section as you would draw it on paper: across to the right (away from the path), up the page. Its leftmost point is at the path, its highest point is the surface; where it doubles back, the lowest part counts. Draw it as construction geometry (so it is never cut itself), anywhere on the canvas. Change the shape, and the moulding follows.

The side and the ends

  • Side: outside or inside a closed path, left or right of an open path as it was drawn. A new moulding takes outside for an outline, inside for a hole, left for an open path.
  • Corners: at a corner turning away from the profile, the moulding turns round it; at a corner turning towards it, the two meet in a mitre, as a mitred frame’s corner.
  • Ends of an open path:
    • square: the full profile to the path’s end, for a moulding that runs off the board’s edge;
    • round: the profile turns a quarter round the path’s end point, as a moulding bit stopped and lifted out leaves it;
    • tapered: the moulding fades into the board over a length, straight or curved, as a stopped flute does. Along the taper the tool rises and falls with the section.

How it cuts

For each distance from the path, the tool goes as deep as its own shape allows without cutting below the profile: Chipload drops the tool’s shape onto the section. Each pass is the path’s offset at that distance, cut at that depth, one stepover apart across the profile, so the finish runs along the moulding. With a step down, a deep profile is cut in levels, each pass no deeper than its level. The tool lifts between passes.

Where the profile has a curve tighter than the tool, or a wall the ball can’t reach (the sides of a bead, a vertical step in a drawn profile), the tool bridges it; a note says how far it stays above the profile there. A smaller ball, or an end mill, reaches further.

The first pass runs on the path itself, so with a ball nose half the tool cuts beyond the path at the profile’s depth there: right for a cove, ogee or round-over at a part’s edge, where beyond the path is waste. A profile that starts at the surface (a bead, a flute, a drawn band) cuts nothing beyond the path.

A new moulding takes the project’s first ball nose (else its first end mill), a 6 mm cove, square ends, the tool’s step down, climb. The report gives the profile’s depth and the number of passes. The setup sheet says, for example, “Moulding, cove R 6, outside, stepover 0.6, climb”.

Command line

chipload op add part.chipload moulding --shapes 1 --profile cove --radius 8
chipload op add part.chipload moulding --shapes 2 --profile ogee --width 12 --depth 6 --side right
chipload op add part.chipload moulding --shapes 3 --ends taper --taper 30 --ramp-shape curved
chipload op add part.chipload moulding --shapes 4 --profile-shape 7 --ends round
chipload set part.chipload operations.0.profile.radius=6

--side is outside, inside, left or right; --stepover sets the stepover. A moulding takes no --through.

In the file

"type": "moulding" in the project file. Lengths are in mm, feeds in mm/min. A project with a moulding opens in Chipload 0.37.0 and later.
